Questions worth exploring

  1. Does wastewater beat the sick parade for norovirus? CDC describes norovirus symptoms starting 12 to 48 hours after exposure, and they are sudden and hard to hide. On a training site, the first vomiting recruit may reach the medical officer before a sample reaches a lab. Wastewater's lead over clinical reporting is largest for infections that spread silently, such as COVID-19 or influenza. What is the lead time for norovirus, in hours, at your site?
  2. What does your own burden source say causes the illness? Toriro and colleagues found that contact with a colleague who had diarrhoea was the strongest risk factor in both Kenya and Oman, and that in Kenya eating locally sourced food and swimming in local water also raised risk; about half the Kenya cases fell in the first 14 days. That points at prevention as much as detection. The paper appears in BMJ Military Health with a January 2025 publication date, though your deck cites it as 2026; check which version you are citing.
  3. Is there a sewer? Your burden figures come from six-week exercises in Kenya and Oman, which may rely on field sanitation, while your sponsor suggested training sites first and ships later. A sewered barracks at home, a field latrine abroad, and a ship's holding tank are three different sampling problems. Which catchment can you actually reach this semester?
  4. What decision changes, and who makes it? For norovirus the usual response is isolation, cleaning, and hygiene. If the medical officer does those things as soon as the first cases appear, a wastewater reading has to trigger something earlier or different to be worth its cost. Name the person and the decision.
  5. What happens after two false alarms? Your Week 9 deck reported a positive predictive value of 20 percent on the day of diagnosis at small Syracuse sites. A barracks block is a small site. If a reading pauses training and nothing follows, the next reading may be ignored (R3). A small catchment can also identify a unit, which raises privacy questions your WHO sources discuss.
  6. What gap does Untap leave, and for whom? Untap already offers site-level qPCR, a same-day dashboard, baselines and anomaly flags, in defence settings. Your sponsor asked you not to reproduce it, and a startup entering a market where a vendor already sells this needs a reason customers would switch. Treat your sponsor as one customer: ask them, and two or three other potential customers such as a university health service or a ship's medical officer, what Untap does not do for them: cost, ships, operating effort, or something else.
  7. Who keeps it running after the pilot? R2 can run downward. Your Week 9 deck noted the proposed cut to CDC's wastewater budget. At a training site, the sampling falls to staff who rotate. Who owns the monitoring in year two?

What this map leaves out

  • It treats all pathogens alike. Norovirus, adenovirus, influenza and COVID-19 differ in incubation, shedding and how obvious the symptoms are, which changes the value of an early signal.
  • It shows one site. Training establishments, exercises abroad and ships each have different plumbing, staff and chains of command.
  • Clinical surveillance is drawn as one box. In practice it includes sick parade, ward reporting and laboratory confirmation, each with its own delay.
  • I did not check the Untap claims or the Syracuse figure; both come from your decks.
