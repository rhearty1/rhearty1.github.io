Building the map, one layer at a time

Your Week 11 map drew five loops at once. Here is the same system built up from its simplest form. Each layer adds a few boxes, drawn in blue, and says what they change. A box stays only if it sits on a loop or is the outcome we care about, and each arrow says whether we know it or only suppose it. Trace the signs yourself: a loop with an even number of minus signs is reinforcing (R), an odd number is balancing (B).

Layer 0. The fix

A shift empties, the coordinator calls someone in, and the gap closes. One loop with one minus sign: balancing, and fast, working within hours. This is the loop your project lives in. On its own it says the system works.

Layer 1. The side effect

Every call-in adds hours awake and cuts into recovery sleep. Sleep debt is a stock: it builds up and drains only with sleep. If it raises absences weeks later, the fix makes the next gap. No minus signs, so R1 is reinforcing, and slow. Together B1 and R1 form a pattern Peter Senge called fixes that fail: on any one night B1 wins, over a season R1 can. The dashed arrow is the hinge of the whole map. Your own key source, Djupedal and colleagues (2025), measured sickness absence; test this link first.

Layer 2. The body clock, and the harm

Night work adds sleep debt even without call-ins, because sleep taken in daylight runs against the body clock and is shorter and more broken; the Very Short Introduction on circadian rhythms explains why. And sleep debt matters for more than absences. At the body clock's low point, fatigue slows reaction and judgment: the Olivia boiler accident happened at 02:55. Neither box is on a loop. Night work drives the system from outside; errors and injuries are the outcome the loops explain.

Layer 3. Your own loops: lending and standby

Two loops from your deck. When cover is borrowed from another team, that team runs short, and its gap comes back as a request (R2, reinforcing). When the same gaps recur, someone reviews them and builds standby capacity, which prevents later gaps (B2, balancing). Compare the speeds: B1 acts in hours, R1 and R2 in weeks, B2 in months. Fast loops win the night; slow loops decide the year.

Layer 4. Information and inflow

Two places to push. If the coordinator can see each worker's recent hours and rest, the call goes to someone rested, and each call-in adds less sleep debt: the minus sign weakens R1 without weakening B1. That is your leverage point, an information flow, high on Donella Meadows' ladder. The roster and vacancy rate act earlier still, on the inflow of gaps before anyone picks up the phone. A 2025 Southampton study linked understaffing in the previous week to more nurses reporting sick, which feeds R1 from the other side.

Questions worth exploring

  1. Where do the unexpected gaps come from? Your own key source, Djupedal and colleagues (2025), measured sickness absence as its outcome. A 2025 Southampton study of about 2.5 million shifts linked understaffing in the previous week to more registered nurses reporting sick, and weeks of all long shifts to 26 percent higher odds. If gaps are produced by the roster and the vacancy rate, the call-in decision treats a symptom (R3).
  2. Is rest only a number of hours? Your rules count rest in hours: under 11 between shifts is a short rest. Sleep science adds timing. A worker called in overnight is awake during their biological night and must then sleep during the day, against their body clock, so two eleven-hour rests may not be equal. Russell Foster and Leon Kreitzman's Circadian Rhythms: A Very Short Introduction explains why. Which box or loop in your map would change if rest were measured as sleep at the right time?
  3. Can you get the records? Your replay probe needs a de-identified past call-in and what the coordinator saw. Which hospital, which coordinator, and what permission would you need? Without the records, the probe cannot run.
  4. Is it missing information or missing authority? HSSIB found that managers may not see extra shifts worked elsewhere and may override fatigue warnings. If coordinators already see the warning and approve anyway, better information will not change the choice. Your probe's reading rules can tell these apart; say which result you expect.
  5. Is approval where the choice is made? In many offices the first person reached who says yes gets the shift. The order of calls, made before your approval step, may decide the outcome. Watch one coordinator work a gap.
  6. Why the ship? The Olivia case is vivid, and you note that its mechanism differs from a hospital call-in. A hospital incident would make the same point with the users you mean to serve.
  7. Why do workers say yes? Pay, loyalty, guilt or pressure? The worker is a decision-maker too, and the one whose rest is lost. Talk to two nurses who take extra shifts.
