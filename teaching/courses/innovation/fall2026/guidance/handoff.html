Building the map, one layer at a time

Your map has many factors. Underneath them is one loop: someone gets worse, help is called, care arrives. Here it is, then the steps that slow it. Each layer adds a few boxes, drawn in blue, and says what they change. A box stays only if it sits on a loop or is the outcome we care about, and each arrow says whether we know it or only suppose it. Trace the signs yourself: a loop with an even number of minus signs is reinforcing (R), an odd number is balancing (B).

Layer 0. Getting help

Someone gets worse; someone calls; care arrives and the worsening stops. One loop, balancing. Everything in your project is about the delays on these arrows. The question is which delay is longest.

Layer 1. Before the call

The first arrow splits. Before anyone calls, someone has to notice that a change matters, and that depends on what they know: training for a teammate, discharge teaching for a caregiver. In your own leg-injury example, a caregiver who does not know new tingling matters never calls, and no card can help. This delay may be longer than any after the call.

Layer 2. The report itself

This is where your report card works. Missing details prompt follow-up questions, which fill the gaps: B2, your own balancing loop. A structured card cuts missing details before the questions start. But military medicine already has the MIST report, and nurse lines run on structured protocols; both act on the same arrow. Your card has to do something they do not.

Layer 3. Workload

Every follow-up adds to the clinician's workload, workload slows the response, and slow responses make people call again: R1, reinforcing, from your deck. A delay here also means the person keeps getting worse while they wait, so R1 feeds back into the first layer. Your card relieves R1 only if it reduces follow-ups.

Questions worth exploring

  1. What do existing formats already do? Military casualty reporting already has a structure: the 9-line request for evacuation, followed by a MIST report for each casualty, covering mechanism, injuries, symptoms and treatment. Civilian nurse lines work from triage protocols that ask structured questions. Does your card add something these do not, or are they not used when a teammate gets worse hours later? Either answer is a finding.
  2. One user or two? A soldier radioing a medic and a family caregiver phoning a surgical nurse line differ in training, routing, stakes and who answers. A probe that mixes them may show nothing for either. Which one first?
  3. Is noticing the weakest step? Your factor list starts with symptom ambiguity. In your own leg-injury example, the risk is that the caregiver does not know new tingling matters, and so never reports it. That is a recognition problem, before any report exists.
  4. Does a structured report shorten the call, or duplicate the protocol? If the nurse asks protocol questions anyway, a pre-filled card may save little time. Ask two triage nurses how they would use it.
  5. Where is the delay? R1 assumes the delay happens after the call. People often wait before calling at all, unsure whether it is serious enough. Which delay is longer in the cases you can find?
  6. Have you met the guideline owner? Your Week 9 next step was to review one symptom pathway with the guideline owner and a clinical adviser. What did they say about leverage point 4?

What this map leaves out

  • It joins two settings, the field and the home, that may need separate maps.
  • The patient's own voice is missing. A conscious patient often reports for themselves.
  • I have not checked how 9-line and MIST reports are used for deterioration after first aid, as opposed to initial casualty reports.
