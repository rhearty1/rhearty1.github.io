A rule for the course: a sponsor is one customer, not a client

Some projects began with a problem handed over by a sponsor. In Innovation and Design, a sponsor is not a client you deliver for. Treat a sponsor as one customer: a user you can reach easily and another pair of eyes on your work. Their problem may not be yours. You are working toward a business, which needs many customers, not a deliverable for one organization that ends when it is handed over. Ask a sponsor what you would ask any customer, then ask two or three others the same questions, and follow the evidence even if it leads away from the problem you were handed.

Main takeaway for all teams

Slow down! Draw more diagrams. Talk more among yourselves and with people connected to your problem space. Read research articles, talk again, go back and read more, talk again, then draw updated diagrams. By Monday, wrap it all up for us in a brief report of what you did. This is close to the pattern of work you will experience every week next semester.

Why I keep asking you to slow down

When Cynthia Atman and her colleagues compared how student and expert engineers worked on the same design problem, one of the largest differences was problem scoping: experts spent more time understanding the problem and gathered more information, across more kinds of sources, before settling on a design. Students tend to move to a solution quickly. That is not a character flaw; it is what novices do, and it is learnable.

A pattern across this week's submissions: the research is mostly reading, and much of it arrives after the solution has been chosen. The maps help because each one shows something upstream of the team's box. Interviews with people who have the problem are the fastest way to test whether your box is in the right place.

System map or process map?

Both. The middle row of each system map is already a process: the path a signal, a patient or a gap takes. The process map zooms into that row and adds who does each step and how long each handoff takes. The system map shows why a problem persists; the process map shows where the time and the handoffs go. Start with one real case as a process, then draw the loops that explain it.

Each team page now opens by building its system map one layer at a time: the simplest loop first, then a few boxes at a time, each layer explained. Draw yours the same way. If you cannot say in a sentence what a new box adds, leave it out until you can.

Eight moves to slow the rush to a solution

  1. Use your map as an interview instrument. Show your system map to three people inside the system, at least one of them someone who has the problem, and ask: what is wrong, what is missing, where would you push? Revise the map and note who changed it. A map is easier to argue with than a blank page, and it gives you a reason to ask for the meeting.
  2. Walk one real case before drawing loops. Build a process map from one documented incident or one person's day, with times. It shows where the delays and handoffs are, and it tests the loops you have guessed.
  3. Place your idea on the leverage ladder. Donella Meadows ranks places to intervene from numbers and buffers, through delays and feedback, to information flows, rules, goals and paradigms. A faster sensor works on delays; who sees the result, and what they are allowed to do with it, sit higher. Ask what the information-flow or rule version of your idea would be.
  4. Ask why three times, then redraw. Each answer moves your box upstream. If the box moves, your problem statement should too.
  5. Name one person and one moment. Who wakes up with this problem, when exactly does it bite, and what do they do today instead? The workaround is evidence that the problem is real.
  6. Strike the solution words. Delete every device, app, AI, dashboard or sensor from your problem statement. If what remains still says something, you have a problem statement; if not, you have a solution in disguise.
  7. Mark every link known, supposed, or to find out. Draw sourced links solid and supposed links dashed, and turn each dashed link into a research task. Your research then has a list.
  8. State your kill result in advance. What finding would make you drop this idea? Writing it down now protects you from defending a solution after the evidence turns.

A note on process. Feedback works best when you can see how it was made. I read each team's deck and the teaching team's summary of it, drew the wider system the problem sits in from the team's sources and a few of my own, and added a process map of one case. I marked each team's current frame in blue and asked of everything else on the map: is anything upstream causing what you are trying to fix, and is anything beside it a bigger lever? I wrote what I found as numbered questions rather than corrections, because you know your problem better than I do. The feedback follows the three questions John Hattie and Helen Timperley found matter most: where am I going, how am I going, and where to next? I drafted the maps with help from an AI assistant and checked them against your decks and the listed sources; where I read only an abstract, the page says so. Treat each map as a draft to argue with.
