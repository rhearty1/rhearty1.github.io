Building the map, one layer at a time

Your deck drew one stock, strength, with loading in and setbacks out. Here is that idea as the simplest loop, then what acts on it. Each layer adds a few boxes, drawn in blue, and says what they change. A box stays only if it sits on a loop or is the outcome we care about, and each arrow says whether we know it or only suppose it. Trace the signs yourself: a loop with an even number of minus signs is reinforcing (R), an odd number is balancing (B).

Layer 0. Rehab as a thermostat

The bigger the strength deficit, the more loading the therapist prescribes; loading shrinks the deficit. One minus sign: balancing. Milestone tests are the thermometer. This is your loop, and on its own it says rehab finishes itself.

Layer 1. Fear

A weak knee feels unsafe; fear makes patients hold back on the loading; less loading leaves the knee weak. Two minus signs: reinforcing. In Ardern's 2011 meta-analysis, fear of re-injury was the reason patients most often gave for not returning. If R1 is strong, a better thermometer for B1 may not be the fix.

Layer 2. Where your device sits

A strength tracker makes progress visible, and you suppose visible progress raises effort: dashed, because that is your hypothesis to test. Trace R2: a shrinking deficit shows progress, which raises loading, which shrinks the deficit further. Reinforcing loops run both ways, so a flat trend may lower effort just as surely. Supervised visits, set largely by insurance and access, decide how much loading happens with a therapist watching; the rest happens at home, where your device would live.

Layer 3. Return, and re-injury

Returning with a deficit raises the risk of re-injury, and re-injury widens the deficit (R3). Pressure from a season or a team pulls the return date earlier. Fear pushes it later, and that closes B2: fear delays return, which lowers re-injury risk. Fear is partly protective. A product that simply removes fear could strengthen R3.

Questions worth exploring

  1. Is strength what holds patients back? Your deck cites Ardern's figures: 81 percent return to some sport, 65 percent to their pre-injury level, 55 percent to competitive sport. In Ardern's earlier meta-analysis, the most common reason patients gave for not returning or for playing less was fear of re-injury, not weakness. If fear is the bigger stock, does a number on a screen help, or does a flat trend make fear worse?
  2. Where does supervision stop? Insurance and access may decide how many visits a patient gets, which pushes most of the loading home. Find out how many PT visits a typical ACL patient actually has and how far apart they are. That gap is the space your device would live in.
  3. What do clinics already measure? Clinics test quadriceps strength at milestones with dynamometers. Is the missing piece home measurement, the trend between visits, or the conversation about what the number means? Each points to a different product.
  4. Who decides the return date, and under what pressure? Guidelines call for criteria-based clearance, but a coach, a season and a scholarship push on the calendar. Who actually sets the date for a high-school or college athlete?
  5. What happens when the trend is flat? Your causal map says tracking raises confidence and so loading. A flat or falling trend could raise fear and cut loading instead (R1). Which way does it go for real patients?
  6. What does re-injury cost, and who bears it? R2 runs from an early return back to injury and a second operation. Ask a surgeon or physical therapist how often they see it and what they think causes it.

What this map leaves out

  • It shows a young athlete. Older or recreational patients, and those treated without surgery, follow different paths.
  • Graft type, meniscus injury and other clinical details are not drawn.
  • I have not checked how often re-injury occurs; the R2 loop marks a question, not a rate.
  • The insurer lane is a guess about the US system; check it with a therapist.
