Questions worth exploring

  1. How does motion compare with what the crew already manages? Military TBI guidelines name hypoxia and hypotension as the main drivers of secondary brain injury. A 2022 study of 404 TBI patients flown by Critical Care Air Transport Teams found hemodynamic events in every phase of flight. Where does motion rank among the insults a brain meets in transit?
  2. Are the transfers the bigger dose? Your deck already flags Blakeman (2013). His Air Force study recorded intracranial pressure during flights from Bagram to Landstuhl and during in-hospital moves. A related 2015 paper attributes pressure changes to vibration, noise, and acceleration. Loading, unloading, and vehicle swaps sit outside your 2–20 Hz in-flight frame.
  3. Is posture a variable alongside vibration? In a 2018 Dutch helicopter study of healthy volunteers, optic nerve sheath diameter, a stand-in for intracranial pressure, rose during acceleration when the head was 9° down. It did not rise with the headrest raised 20–25°. Head angle on the litter may matter as much as the frequency band.
  4. What does existing animal work say about Assumption 1? A 2020 Military Medicine study in pigs found that moderate vibration and hypoxia simulating aeromedical evacuation did not affect short-term outcomes after TBI. How does that sit with the hypothesis that extra motion harms an injured brain? What would your bench rig need to show that this study didn't?
  5. Which evacuations will the number describe? Planning for large-scale combat expects contested airspace, longer evacuations, and more casualties moved in non-standard vehicles without en route care. Longer exposure, other vehicles, and prolonged care at Role 1 may change which motion matters.
  6. Who decides what can fly? Medical equipment on Army aircraft needs an Airworthiness Release, after testing by the Army Aeromedical Research Laboratory under the Joint En Route Care Equipment Test Standard. Your "unpowered sensors only" constraint comes from this institution. Talking to the people who run it may open options or explain the limits.
  7. Whose decision changes once the number exists? The B1 loop shows guideline writers learning from outcomes, and R1 shows standards that cover only comfort for supine patients. Who would actually use a head-exposure specification: standards committees, litter designers, crews, or the sponsor's biomarker lab? Talking to one of each could sharpen the "in order to" part of your statement.

What this map leaves out

  • It shows a US/NATO helicopter pathway. Ground-only, maritime, and long fixed-wing legs each have different vehicles, crews, and exposures.
  • The order of the middle row is simplified. Real evacuations often loop back through Role 1 or Role 2 before neurosurgery.
  • Patients differ (injury severity, sedation, other wounds), and that variation is not drawn.
  • I read some sources only as abstracts or search excerpts, including the porcine study, the Dutch study, and the 2015 paper. Check the full texts before relying on them.
